## Why it matters

Almost every practical question about LLMs — why it hallucinated, why the output changed, whether to fine-tune — follows from understanding that the model predicts tokens, not facts.

## A worked example

```python
import math
from collections import Counter

# The whole idea, in miniature: score every candidate next token,
# then sample from the resulting distribution.
counts = Counter("the the the cat sat".split())
total = sum(counts.values())

logits = {word: math.log(count / total) for word, count in counts.items()}
probs = {word: math.exp(lp) for word, lp in logits.items()}
normalised = {w: p / sum(probs.values()) for w, p in probs.items()}

print(normalised)   # a distribution over "the", "cat", "sat"
```

A real model computes this over a vocabulary of tens of thousands of tokens, using learned weights instead of counts. Everything else — temperature, sampling, why output varies — builds on this distribution.

<Callout type="interview" title="Interview corner">
**Why do language models hallucinate?**

Because the training objective rewards producing plausible text, not true text. A model that has never seen a fact still has a distribution over how that fact might be worded, and sampling from it produces a fluent, confident, wrong answer. There is no internal signal that distinguishes "I know this" from "this is what such text usually says".
</Callout>

## Quick recap

- A language model predicts the next token; everything else follows.
- Pretraining teaches patterns, not facts. Facts come from retrieval or tuning.
- Hallucination is a consequence of the objective, not a bug to be patched.
