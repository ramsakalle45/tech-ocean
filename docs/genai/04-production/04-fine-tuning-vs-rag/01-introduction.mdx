## What you will learn

- Distinguish tuning for behaviour from retrieval for knowledge
- Decide which approach fits your problem, with reasons
- Understand the operational cost of each
- Plan a change with the fewest irreversible steps

## Why it matters

This is the question that comes up most often in design review, and the answer is usually not "the newer technique". Choosing wrong means either paying for stale knowledge or fighting a model that will not behave.

## A worked example

```text
Question: "Summarise this support ticket in our house style."
  -> Tuning helps: the style is behaviour, and the input is given.

Question: "What is our refund policy for annual contracts?"
  -> Retrieval helps: the policy is knowledge, it changes, and it
     must be citable and current.

Question: "Classify this ticket into one of 40 categories, every time."
  -> Tuning helps if the categories are stable and the volume is high;
     retrieval does not apply at all.

Question: "Answer questions about our 200-page handbook, with citations."
  -> Retrieval, almost certainly.
```

The dividing line is simple: is the thing you are changing a fact, or a behaviour?

<Callout type="interview" title="Interview corner">
**A team wants to fine-tune so their model knows their product. What would you ask?**

What exactly is missing? If it is factual knowledge that changes weekly, tuning is the wrong tool — you would be baking a snapshot into weights that goes stale and cannot be cited or withdrawn. Retrieval gives fresh, citable, permission-controlled knowledge. Tuning is for behaviour: output format, tone, classification, and following a process you can demonstrate with examples.
</Callout>

## Quick recap

- Facts that change → retrieval. Behaviour that must be consistent → tuning.
- Start with prompting, then retrieval, then tuning.
- Tuning is the most expensive and hardest to reverse of the three.
