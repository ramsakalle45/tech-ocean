## What you will learn

- Estimate storage, bandwidth and request rates from first principles
- Use powers of two and orders of magnitude instead of false precision
- Know which estimates the design actually depends on
- State assumptions and let them be corrected

## Why it matters

Estimation is not a test of arithmetic. It is a test of whether you can work out what matters, and it is the fastest way to look competent in the first five minutes of an interview.

## A worked example

```text
Numbers worth having in your head:
  1 KB = 10^3 bytes        1 GB = 10^9 bytes
  1 MB = 10^6 bytes        1 TB = 10^12 bytes
  1 day  = 86,400 s        1 year ≈ 3.15 × 10^7 s
  1 user ≈ 10 requests/day (rough social-app default)

Worked example — a URL shortener:
  100M users × 10 URLs each per year = 10^9 URLs/year
  each row ≈ 100 bytes (key, url, user, timestamps)
  → 10^9 × 100 B ≈ 100 GB/year  ≈ 1 TB after 10 years

  Read path: if 20% of users visit daily and each visit
  resolves 5 short links:
  100M × 0.2 × 5 = 100M redirects/day ≈ 1,200 QPS average
  peak maybe 3–5× that → design for ~5,000 QPS

Say this out loud: "about a terabyte a decade for
 storage, and a few thousand requests per second at peak."
```

Order-of-magnitude answers are correct answers. A number with three significant figures is usually a sign you are faking precision.

<Callout type="interview" title="Interview corner">
**How many requests per second do you need to design for?**

From daily active users, requests per user per day, and the concentration of traffic into the peak hours. If a hundred million users each make ten requests a day, that is a billion requests a day, which is around 11,600 per second on average. Peak is typically three to five times the average, so I would design for roughly 50,000 requests per second — and I would check the traffic distribution, because a system with a strong daily cycle is very different from one that is flat.
</Callout>

## Quick recap

- Memorise the powers of two, not the formulas.
- Design for peak, and say your peak assumption.
- Round aggressively and be explicit that you are rounding.
